# Theories and what counts as evidence

For each theory: what it says in one line, what counts as evidence in a session, and common misreadings to avoid.

## Adult learning principles (Knowles, andragogy)
**Idea:** adults learn best when learning is relevant to their own work, problem-centered, draws on their experience, and gives them some self-direction.
**Evidence:** activities start from learners' real tasks or problems; learners bring their own examples; learners make choices about what or how they learn.
**Avoid:** claiming it just because the audience was adults.

## Experiential learning (Kolb)
**Idea:** learning happens in a cycle: concrete experience, reflection, forming ideas, trying again.
**Evidence:** learners do something, review the result, and adjust, ideally more than once (e.g. test an agent, review the output, refine the instructions).
**Avoid:** claiming it for a demo learners only watched.

## Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan)
**Idea:** motivation grows when people feel *autonomy* (choice), *competence* (a real win), and *relatedness* (connection to others).
**Evidence:** name which of the three each design choice supports, e.g. a vote (autonomy), a working result in the session (competence), sharing with peers (relatedness).
**Avoid:** claiming all three without a choice for each. Use the theory's own terms: *autonomy*, *competence*, *relatedness* (not "connection").

## Cognitive load theory / worked examples (Sweller)
**Idea:** working memory is limited; worked examples, templates, and removing unnecessary steps free attention for the actual learning.
**Evidence:** templates, example answers, ready-made prompts, chunked steps, deliberately cutting non-essential content.
**Avoid:** claiming it just because the session was short. A question shown *before* a video (to tell people what to watch for) is closer to signaling or a pre-question than to cognitive load; two reviewers flagged that in Session 2, so don't use this theory for it.

## Scaffolding (Vygotsky's zone of proximal development; Wood, Bruner & Ross)
**Idea:** support learners to do what they can't yet do alone, then remove the support over time.
**Evidence:** guided steps that fade (or a plan to fade them), e.g. examples first, then learners choose their own use cases.
**Avoid:** claiming fading if the support never decreased; say "with a plan to fade" if that's only in "what I'd do next".

## Social learning (Bandura) / communities of practice (Lave & Wenger)
**Idea:** people learn by watching and interacting with others; communities sustain learning over time.
**Evidence:** peers sharing on mic, learning from each other's examples, an ongoing peer-learning series, people helping each other after the session.
**Avoid:** calling a single session a community of practice.

## Psychological safety (Edmondson)
**Idea:** people speak up, ask questions, and take risks when they believe they won't be embarrassed or punished for it.
**Evidence:** deliberate choices that lower the risk of being wrong, e.g. a topic with no right answer, anonymous polls, normalizing questions.
**Avoid:** claiming people *felt* safe without evidence; describe the design choice and its intent.

## ADKAR (Prosci)
**Idea:** individual change needs Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement, in that order.
**Evidence, per stage:**
- *Awareness:* helping people understand **why the change is needed** (the business reason). Often comes from leadership, not the session.
- *Desire:* building personal motivation to take part ("what's in it for me", reducing fear, choice).
- *Knowledge:* knowing how to make the change (instruction, demonstration). Concepts about how AI works are not *Knowledge*; a concept-only session with a balanced "why it matters" view is honestly an *Awareness* session (Session 2).
- *Ability:* actually doing it, with practice, ideally on real work.
- *Reinforcement:* sustaining the change (recognition, follow-up, check-ins, manager accountability).
**Avoid:** "what's in it for me" as Awareness (it's Desire); resources or recordings as Reinforcement (they're support); claiming all five stages from one session.

## Kirkpatrick's four levels of evaluation
**Idea:** evaluate training at four levels: 1 reaction, 2 learning, 3 behavior on the job, 4 business results.
**Evidence, per level:**
- *Level 1, reaction:* participant feedback, quotes, requests for more.
- *Level 2, learning:* learners demonstrate the skill (e.g. built a working agent in the session).
- *Level 3, behavior:* learners use it in real work afterwards (e.g. a team using the method months later).
- *Level 4, results:* measurable business outcomes.
**Avoid:** claiming Level 4 without business data; calling one anecdote "measured" behavior change (say what was observed).
**Note:** two of Teresa's target roles name Kirkpatrick. When the evidence is honest, naming the levels is valuable.

## Bloom's taxonomy (revised, Anderson & Krathwohl)
**Idea:** learning objectives range from remember and understand up to apply, analyze, evaluate, and create.
**Evidence:** objectives use observable action verbs; activities match the level (e.g. "build" and "refine" are apply/create).
**Sequencing (Teresa's rule): foundation before explanation.** Learners need information before they can explain, evaluate, or create. A first or introductory session usually belongs at *remember* and *understand* (describe, identify), with first steps toward *analyze*. Flag objectives that ask for *explain* or *evaluate* when the session only introduced the material.
**Use mainly** to check that learning objectives are measurable and pitched at the right level. It can be a "Learning foundations" line when the sequencing was a deliberate choice.
