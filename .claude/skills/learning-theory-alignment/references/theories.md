# Theories and what counts as evidence

**This is a closed list.** Only claim the eight theories below, plus Teresa's own practices at the end of this file. If a session seems to fit a theory that isn't here, tell Teresa and suggest adding a source first. Don't cite it from memory.

Each entry gives the source, the idea in a line or two, what counts as evidence in a single workshop, and misreadings to avoid. Page numbers are the printed page numbers in each source. The fuller notes these entries come from are in `content/theory-guide-claude.md`.

**Citing.** Cite the source listed, not a textbook from memory. Knowles is read through Clardy, so write "Knowles (1980), as cited in Clardy (2005)." On the portfolio, theory names alone are enough ("Self-determination theory"); full citations are for Teresa's reference and interview prep.

## Adult learning principles (Knowles, andragogy)
**Source:** Clardy, A. (2005). *Andragogy: Adult learning and education at its best?* ERIC ED492132. Knowles's six assumptions, pp. 5–7; principles, p. 10.
**Idea:** adults learn best when learning is relevant to real problems, draws on their experience, explains why it matters, and gives them some say in what and how they learn.
**Evidence:**
- Activities start from learners' real tasks or problems, and the session says why the topic matters.
- Learners bring and use their own examples.
- Learners shape what or how they learn (they chose topics, voted on format, set their own use case). Clardy treats learner input into planning as the core of andragogy (pp. 12–13), so this is the strongest evidence.
- The facilitator asks and routes questions rather than only lecturing.
**Avoid:**
- Claiming it just because the audience was adults.
- Treating hands-on activity alone as andragogy; experiential activities fit traditional teaching too (p. 12).
- Saying it *caused* better learning. Research on andragogy is mixed: satisfaction often rises, learning gains are inconsistent (pp. 21–27). "Aligned with" is the honest framing.
- Assuming all adults want self-directed learning; many prefer instructor-set content (p. 18).
- Partial wording that works: "aligned with Knowles's assumptions about relevance and experience" when learners didn't help plan.

## Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci)
**Source:** Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78.
**Idea:** motivation grows when three needs are supported: *autonomy* (a sense of choice), *competence* (a real win), and *relatedness* (connection to others) (p. 68).
**Evidence:** name which need each design choice supports:
- *Autonomy:* a real choice (a vote, choosing a use case). Choice and acknowledging feelings support it (p. 70).
- *Competence:* a challenge people could meet, with a working result or useful feedback in the session (p. 70).
- *Relatedness:* structured connection with peers (sharing, discussion) (p. 71).
- For required content: explaining *why* it matters helps people take it on as their own (internalization, pp. 71–74).
**Avoid:**
- Claiming all three without a design choice for each. Use the theory's own terms: *autonomy*, *competence*, *relatedness* (not "connection").
- Equating autonomy with working alone. In SDT it means volition, not independence (p. 74).
- Citing rewards, badges, or deadlines as SDT evidence; those tend to undermine intrinsic motivation (p. 70).
- Saying people were "intrinsically motivated" by required content; "supported internalization" is more accurate.

## Cognitive load theory / worked examples (Sweller)
**Source:** Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. G. W. C. (1998). Cognitive architecture and instructional design. *Educational Psychology Review, 10*(3), 251–296.
**Idea:** working memory is limited. Good design cuts *extraneous* load (caused by how material is presented) and supports *germane* load (effort that builds understanding) (p. 259).
**Evidence:** worked examples or templates (pp. 273–275); partly completed tasks learners finish, e.g. a half-built prompt (pp. 275–277); instructions placed with the tool or example rather than split across screens (pp. 277–281); cutting content that repeats what's already shown (pp. 283–286); varied practice examples (pp. 286–289).
**Avoid:**
- Claiming it just because the session was short.
- Treating the goal as removing all effort; effort that builds understanding is the point (pp. 259, 264).
- Applying it to simple material; these effects mainly matter when material is complex (p. 291).
- Saying the design "reduced intrinsic load"; in this source, intrinsic load can't be changed by design (p. 259).
- A question shown *before* a video (to tell people what to watch for) is closer to signaling or a pre-question than to cognitive load; two reviewers flagged that in Session 2, so don't use this theory for it.

## Psychological safety (Edmondson)
**Source:** Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350–383.
**Idea:** a shared belief in a group that it's safe to take interpersonal risks: to ask questions, admit mistakes, and speak up without being embarrassed or punished (pp. 350, 354). It enables learning behaviors like seeking feedback, asking for help, and talking about errors (p. 353).
**Evidence:**
- Deliberate choices that lower the risk of being wrong: a topic with no single right answer, anonymous polls, normalizing questions, the facilitator modeling not-knowing or responding non-defensively (p. 356).
- Observed learning behaviors: people asked questions, admitted confusion, or shared what didn't work.
**Avoid:**
- Claiming people *felt* safe without evidence; describe the design choice and its intent.
- Saying a session "created" psychological safety. It's a shared belief built over a group's history (p. 379); a session can *support* it.
- Equating it with comfort or niceness (p. 354), or treating it as an individual trait rather than a shared climate (pp. 354–355).
- Forgetting the original setting was ongoing work teams, not one-off workshop groups.

## ADKAR (Prosci)
**Source:** Prosci. *The Prosci ADKAR Model* (web page, prosci.com/methodology/adkar). A model for individual change, not a complete change-management methodology.
**Idea:** individual change needs Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement, in that order. The earliest element that's missing is the *barrier point*.
**Evidence, per stage:**
- *Awareness:* helping people understand **why the change is needed** (the business reason). Often comes from leadership, not the session.
- *Desire:* building personal motivation to take part ("what's in it for me", reducing fear, choice). Desire is a personal choice, so a session can *address* it but shouldn't claim it was achieved without evidence (e.g. people volunteering to continue).
- *Knowledge:* knowing how to make the change (instruction, demonstration). Concepts about how AI works are not *Knowledge*; a concept-only session with a balanced "why it matters" view is honestly an *Awareness* session (Session 2).
- *Ability:* actually doing it, with practice, ideally on real work.
- *Reinforcement:* sustaining the change after the session (recognition, follow-up, check-ins, manager accountability).
**Avoid:**
- "What's in it for me" as Awareness (it's Desire).
- Resources or recordings as Reinforcement (they're support).
- Claiming all five stages from one session. "Strongest at Awareness and Knowledge, with first practice toward Ability" is honest.
- Treating ADKAR as an instructional design model; it's a change model.

## Kirkpatrick's four levels (New World Kirkpatrick Model)
**Source:** Kirkpatrick, J., & Kirkpatrick, W. K. (2022). *An introduction to the New World Kirkpatrick Model.* Kirkpatrick Partners. Levels overview, p. 4.
**Idea:** evaluate training at four levels (p. 4):
- *Level 1, reaction:* whether participants find it favorable, engaging, and relevant to their jobs (p. 9).
- *Level 2, learning:* knowledge, skills, attitude, **confidence** ("I think I can do it on the job"), and **commitment** ("I will do it on the job") (pp. 8–9).
- *Level 3, behavior:* applying it on the job, supported by *required drivers* such as job aids and coaching (p. 7).
- *Level 4, results:* targeted outcomes, tracked through leading indicators (pp. 5–6).
**Evidence:**
- *Level 1:* participant feedback, quotes, requests for more.
- *Level 2:* learners demonstrate the skill (e.g. built a working agent in the session), or an end-of-session check on confidence or commitment.
- *Level 3:* learners use it in real work afterwards (e.g. a team using the method months later).
- *Level 4:* measurable business outcomes.
**Avoid:**
- Claiming a level was measured without data from it. If nothing was collected, say what you'd measure next.
- Claiming Level 4 without business data; calling one anecdote "measured" behavior change (say what was observed).
- The 15%/85% figure (training alone vs. with support) is Brinkerhoff (2006), as cited by the Kirkpatricks (p. 7), not their own research. ROE and "contributive ROI" are not in this source; don't cite them to it.
**Note:** two of Teresa's target roles name Kirkpatrick. When the evidence is honest, naming the levels is valuable.

## Bloom's taxonomy (revised, Anderson & Krathwohl)
**Source:** Krathwohl, D. R. (2002). A revision of Bloom's taxonomy: An overview. *Theory Into Practice, 41*(4), 212–218. Optional summary: Wilson, L. O. (2016), *Anderson and Krathwohl: Bloom's taxonomy revised.*
**Idea:** learning objectives range across six cognitive processes: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create (p. 215). The revision adds a knowledge dimension: factual, conceptual, procedural, and metacognitive (p. 214). The levels overlap rather than form a strict ladder (p. 215).
**Evidence:** objectives use observable action verbs, and activities match the level. For AI sessions: explaining how a model works is *understand*; running a prompt technique is *apply*; checking or critiquing AI output against criteria is *evaluate*; building a new agent or workflow is *create*; reflecting on one's own prompting habits is *metacognitive* knowledge.
**Sequencing (Teresa's rule): foundation before explanation.** Learners need information before they can explain, evaluate, or create. A first or introductory session usually belongs at *remember* and *understand* (describe, identify), with first steps toward *analyze*. Flag objectives that ask for *explain* or *evaluate* when the session only introduced the material.
**Avoid:**
- Labeling by verb alone; check what learners actually did.
- Claiming a high level for something learners only watched.
- Using the revised terms (remember … create) while citing Bloom 1956.
**Use mainly** to check that learning objectives are measurable and pitched at the right level. It can be a "Learning foundations" line when the sequencing was a deliberate choice.

## ADDIE
**Source:** Dousay, T. A. (2018). Instructional design models. In R. E. West (Ed.), *Foundations of Learning and Instructional Design Technology* (ch. 22). EdTech Books. Phase definitions are Branch (2009), as quoted there.
**Idea:** the general process underlying instructional design, not a specific model: **Analyze** the causes of a performance gap, **Design** the desired performances and how to test them, **Develop** and validate materials, **Implement** with learners, **Evaluate** products and process before and after. It's iterative, not a checklist.
**Evidence:** describes how a *series or project* was run, not why one activity worked. Fair: Teresa found out what the team needed, designed and built sessions, ran them, and revised based on what she learned.
**Avoid:**
- Using it as a "Learning foundations" line for a single session.
- "I used ADDIE" unless the phases were deliberate; "followed an ADDIE-style cycle across the series" when they happened informally.
- Claiming an Evaluate phase with no evaluation data.

# Teresa's practices (from her facilitation training)

These are rules of thumb Teresa follows. They are **not** theories and have no source in this file, so:
- Present them as her practice, in her voice: "a practice I follow from my facilitation training."
- Never attribute them to a researcher, or call them research-backed or a theory.
- Only claim she followed one when the session shows it (agenda timings, what learners did).
- They can appear in "Learning foundations" if clearly labeled as practices, or in the narrative of the write-up.

## 75/25 rule
**Practice:** learners do or say about 75% of the session; the facilitator presents about 25%.
**Evidence:** the agenda or recording shows most of the time went to activity, discussion, or practice rather than presentation.
**Avoid:** claiming it for a session that was mostly demo or lecture; giving an exact ratio unless she timed it ("most of the session was hands-on" is fine).

## Show, don't tell
**Practice:** demonstrate the thing (a live prompt, a worked example, a real output) instead of explaining it in the abstract.
**Evidence:** a live demo or worked example came before or instead of explanation.
**Avoid:** counting slides *about* a tool as showing.
**Sourced pairing (optional):** when the "show" is a worked example learners study, the same choice can also be claimed as cognitive load theory's worked-example effect (Sweller et al., 1998, pp. 273–275). The practice itself stays labeled as hers.
