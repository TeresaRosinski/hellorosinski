## 1. Adult learning theory (Knowles's andragogy)

**Source:** Clardy, A. (2005). *Andragogy: Adult learning and education at its best?* Towson University (ERIC ED492132). Knowles's own works are cited *as quoted in Clardy*, so write e.g. "Knowles (1980, p. 44), as cited in Clardy (2005, p. 6)."

**What it says**
- Knowles defined andragogy as the art and science of helping adults learn, resting on assumptions about how adults differ from children as learners. Knowles (1968, p. 351), as cited in Clardy [src p. 4].
- Knowles later softened this: both approaches can suit adults or children "depending on circumstances" (Clardy calls this the *Depends* version) [src p. 4]; educators should check which assumptions are realistic in a given situation. Knowles, Holton & Swanson (1998, p. 69), as cited in Clardy [src p. 8].
- **Six assumptions** about adult learners [src pp. 5–7]: (1) a self-concept of autonomy and self-direction; (2) a reservoir of life experience; (3) a need to understand *why* they are learning something; (4) readiness to learn driven by real needs and problems; (5) a pragmatic, here-and-now orientation; (6) internally driven motivation.
- **Principles** that follow [src p. 10]: learners help define what they learn; planning is shared; learners help evaluate; the climate is safe and non-threatening; learning builds on existing experience; activities are hands-on and experiential; the curriculum is problem-based, not subject-based; the teacher is a facilitator and co-learner.
- What Clardy treats as *essential* (versus common to most good teaching): learner involvement in planning, diagnosing needs, setting objectives, designing, and evaluating, with the teacher acting as facilitator [src pp. 12–13]. These can be present to different degrees [src p. 13].
- Evidence: studies of andragogical methods often found higher satisfaction but inconsistent effects on learning; "weak and inconclusive" overall [src pp. 21–27].
- Limits: workplace training is often mandatory with preset outcomes, which fits andragogy poorly [src p. 14]. Adults also learn from curiosity, not only practical need [src p. 16], and many adults prefer instructor-set content [src p. 18].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- Content tied to problems participants actually face at work (assumptions 4–5), and the session says why it matters (assumption 3).
- Participants bring and use their own examples or experience (assumption 2, principle 5).
- Participants shape what or how they learn, e.g., they chose topics, voted on format, or set their own use case (Clardy's essential features). This is the strongest evidence.
- The facilitator asks and routes questions rather than only lecturing (principle 8).

**Avoid**
- Claiming andragogy because the audience was adults [guidance].
- Treating hands-on activity alone as distinctively andragogical; Clardy notes experiential activities fit traditional teaching too [src p. 12].
- Claiming the approach *caused* better learning; the research is mixed [src p. 27]. "Aligned with" is the honest framing.
- Attributing ideas to Knowles that come from elsewhere (e.g., "lateral knowledge extension" is not Knowles's term) [guidance].
- Assuming all adults want self-directed learning. Adults vary; in one survey Clardy cites, half wanted the instructor to set course content and objectives [src p. 18].
- Partial wording that works: "aligned with Knowles's assumptions about relevance and experience" when learners didn't help plan [guidance].

## 2. Prosci ADKAR

**Source:** Prosci. *The Prosci ADKAR Model* (web page, accessed 2026-11-13). No page numbers. This is a marketing page; consider adding Prosci's downloadable ADKAR guide for more depth.

**What it says** [src, web page]
- ADKAR is a model for **individual** change: organizational change happens one person at a time.
- Five elements: **Awareness** (why the change is needed), **Desire** (choosing to participate and support it), **Knowledge** (how to change), **Ability** (putting new skills and behaviors into practice), **Reinforcement** (sustaining the change and preventing backsliding).
- Progress can be diagnosed by finding the **earliest barrier point**, the first element that isn't in place.
- Prosci describes ADKAR as a model, not a complete change-management methodology.

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- **Awareness:** the session explains why the change matters (e.g., why AI matters for this team, risks included).
- **Knowledge:** the session teaches how to do the new thing.
- **First steps toward Ability:** participants practice it during the session.
- **Desire:** a workshop can *address* desire (relevance, choice, peer stories), but Desire is a personal choice, so don't claim it was achieved without evidence such as participants volunteering to keep going.
- **Reinforcement** happens after and outside a single session (follow-ups, office hours, manager support).

**Avoid**
- Claiming all five elements for one session. "Strongest at Awareness and Knowledge, with first practice toward Ability" is honest [guidance].
- Claiming adoption or completed change after training [guidance].
- Treating ADKAR as an instructional design model; it's a change model [src].
- Skipping the barrier point: teaching *how* to change (Knowledge) when people don't yet see *why* (Awareness) or haven't chosen to support it (Desire) [src, guidance].

## 3. New World Kirkpatrick Model

**Source:** Kirkpatrick, J., & Kirkpatrick, W. K. (2022). *An Introduction to The New World Kirkpatrick Model*. Kirkpatrick Partners. (File dated October 2022 at the 2021 URL; page numbers below match that file.)

**What it says**
- Four levels [src p. 4]:
  - **Level 1 Reaction:** how far participants find the training favorable, engaging, and relevant to their jobs. Three dimensions: customer satisfaction, engagement, relevance [src p. 9].
  - **Level 2 Learning:** knowledge, skills, attitude, **confidence**, and **commitment** [src pp. 4, 8–9]. Confidence and commitment were added to close the gap between learning and on-the-job behavior [src p. 8]. Each has a plain-language test, e.g., confidence is "I think I can do it on the job"; commitment is "I will do it on the job" [src pp. 8–9].
  - **Level 3 Behavior:** applying what was learned back on the job; made up of **critical behaviors**, **required drivers** (systems that reinforce, monitor, encourage, and reward, e.g., job aids, coaching), and **on-the-job learning** [src pp. 7–8].
  - **Level 4 Results:** targeted outcomes from the learning plus reinforcement; tracked through **leading indicators** [src pp. 5–6].
- Levels 1–2 show "effective training"; Levels 3–4 show "training effectiveness" [src p. 10].
- Plan from Level 4 down, not Level 1 up [src p. 10].
- The 15%/85% figure (training alone vs. training with support and accountability) appears here, attributed to Brinkerhoff (2006) [src p. 7]. Cite it as Brinkerhoff via Kirkpatrick, not as Kirkpatrick's own research.
- Poor performance is often wrongly diagnosed as a knowledge or skill gap when the cause is motivation or environment [src p. 8].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- **Level 1** only if reactions were actually collected (a feedback form, a pulse question on relevance).
- **Level 2** only if learning, confidence, or commitment was actually checked (a practice result, an end-of-session confidence question).
- Without collected data, don't claim a level was measured. You *can* say what you'd measure next.

**Avoid**
- Calling a feedback form "a Kirkpatrick evaluation" without saying which level [guidance].
- Claiming Level 3 or 4 without on-the-job or business data [src p. 10, guidance].
- ROE, ROP, and "contributive ROI" are **not in this source**. Don't cite them to it [guidance].

## 4. ADDIE

**Source:** Dousay, T. A. (2018). Instructional design models. In R. E. West (Ed.), *Foundations of Learning and Instructional Design Technology* (1st ed., ch. 22). EdTech Books. Web chapter, no page numbers. Phase definitions are Branch (2009, p. 3), as quoted in Dousay.

**What it says** [src, ch. 22]
- ADDIE is a **process**, not a model: the general sequence underlying instructional design. Specific models are instances of it.
- Phases (Branch, 2009, as quoted): **Analyze** probable causes of a performance gap; **Design** to verify desired performances and testing methods; **Develop** and validate learning resources; **Implement** by preparing the environment and engaging learners; **Evaluate** the quality of products and processes before and after implementation.
- Practitioner advice in the chapter: it's iterative, not a checklist of discrete steps.

**What fairly counts as alignment** [guidance]
- ADDIE describes how a *project* was run, not why an activity works. Use it for the series or program, not for one activity.
- Fair: you found out what the team needed (analysis), designed and built sessions, ran them, and revised based on what you learned (evaluation).

**Avoid**
- "I used ADDIE" unless you deliberately worked in those phases [guidance].
- Claiming an Evaluate phase without any evaluation data [guidance].
- Describing ADDIE as rigid or strictly linear; the chapter frames it as iterative [src].

## 5. Bloom's taxonomy (revised)

**Source:** Krathwohl, D. R. (2002). A revision of Bloom's taxonomy: An overview. *Theory Into Practice, 41*(4), 212–218. (The revision itself is Anderson, Krathwohl, et al., 2001.)

**What it says**
- The taxonomy classifies statements of what learners are expected to learn. Bloom also saw it as a way to check that objectives, activities, and assessments line up [src p. 212].
- The revision has **two dimensions** [src pp. 213–215]:
  - **Knowledge:** factual, conceptual, procedural, and (new) **metacognitive** [src p. 214, Table 2].
  - **Cognitive process:** Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, **Evaluate** (checking, critiquing), **Create** (generating, planning, producing), with 19 specific processes [src p. 215, Table 3].
- It's a hierarchy of complexity, but **not a strict one**; categories overlap (e.g., explaining can be harder than executing) [src p. 215].
- The Taxonomy Table maps objectives, activities, and assessments to show emphasis, alignment, and missed opportunities [src pp. 215–218].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- Name the level the activity actually required. For AI sessions: explaining how a model works is *Understand*; running a prompt technique is *Apply*; checking or critiquing AI output against criteria is *Evaluate*; drafting a new agent or workflow is *Create*.
- Reflection on one's own prompting habits can be *metacognitive knowledge*.

**Avoid**
- Labeling by verb alone; check what learners actually did [guidance].
- Claiming a high level for something learners only watched [guidance].
- Using revised terms (Remember … Create) while citing Bloom 1956 [src p. 214].
- Implying learners must master each level before the next; the revision relaxed that [src p. 215].

## 6. Self-determination theory

**Source:** Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78.

**What it says**
- Three innate psychological needs: **competence, autonomy, relatedness**. When satisfied, motivation and well-being rise; when thwarted, they drop [src p. 68].
- **Competence** is supported by optimal challenges, feedback that builds a sense of capability, and freedom from demeaning evaluation [src p. 70]. But competence alone doesn't raise intrinsic motivation without **autonomy** [src p. 70].
- **Autonomy** is supported by choice, acknowledging feelings, and chances for self-direction. Tangible rewards, threats, deadlines, directives, pressured evaluations, and imposed goals tend to undermine it [src p. 70]. Autonomy means volition, **not** independence or working alone [src p. 74].
- **Relatedness:** intrinsic motivation is more likely with a sense of security and connection; cold or uncaring teachers lower it [src p. 71].
- Intrinsic motivation only applies to activities people find interesting. For required or uninteresting tasks, the question is **internalization** along a continuum: external → introjected → identified → integrated [src pp. 71–73]. A meaningful rationale plus support for autonomy and relatedness promotes internalization [src p. 74].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- **Autonomy:** a real choice (topic, format, own use case).
- **Competence:** a challenge participants could meet, with a working result or useful feedback in the session.
- **Relatedness:** structured connection with peers (sharing, discussion).
- For required content: explaining *why* it matters supports internalization.

**Avoid**
- Claiming all three needs without a specific design choice for each [guidance].
- Equating autonomy with "they worked alone" [src p. 74].
- Pointing to rewards, badges, or deadlines as SDT evidence; the source says those tend to undermine intrinsic motivation [src p. 70].
- Saying participants were "intrinsically motivated" by mandatory content; "supported internalization" is more accurate [src pp. 71–73].

## 7. Cognitive load theory

**Source:** Sweller, J., van Merriënboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. G. W. C. (1998). Cognitive architecture and instructional design. *Educational Psychology Review, 10*(3), 251–296.

**What it says**
- Working memory is very limited (only two or three items can be actively processed at once); long-term memory is effectively unlimited and stores knowledge as **schemas** [src pp. 252, 255–256].
- Three kinds of load [src p. 259]:
  - **Intrinsic:** from how many elements must be processed together; depends on the learner's expertise [src pp. 261–262].
  - **Extraneous:** caused by how instruction is designed; unnecessary.
  - **Germane:** effort that builds schemas; good design reduces extraneous and increases germane load.
- Design effects with evidence [src pp. 270–289]: **worked examples** (pp. 273–275), **completion problems** that fade from full to partial solutions (pp. 275–277), **split attention** (pp. 277–281), **modality** (pp. 281–283), **redundancy** (pp. 283–286), **variability** of practice (pp. 286–289).
- Material that helps novices can be redundant for experts [src pp. 284–285].
- Reducing extraneous load matters most when the material is complex (high element interactivity) [src pp. 281, 291].
- Heavy reliance on worked examples has downsides, e.g., stereotyped solutions [src p. 275].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- Worked examples or templates (a ready-made prompt, a sample agent).
- Completion tasks (a partly built prompt participants finish).
- Integrated materials: instructions placed with the tool or example, not split across screens or handouts.
- Deliberately cutting content that repeats what's already shown.
- Varied practice examples.

**Avoid**
- Claiming cognitive load theory because the session was short [guidance].
- Treating the goal as removing all mental effort. Good design cuts extraneous load and *increases* germane load, the effort that builds understanding [src pp. 259, 264].
- Applying it to simple material; the source says these effects mainly matter for complex material [src p. 291].
- Saying the design "reduced intrinsic load"; in this paper, intrinsic load can't be changed by design, only managed [src pp. 259, 262].
- A question shown *before* a video to focus attention is signaling, not cognitive load (carried over from existing reviewer notes) [guidance].

## 8. Psychological safety

**Source:** Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. *Administrative Science Quarterly, 44*(2), 350–383.

**What it says**
- **Team psychological safety** is a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking [src pp. 350, 354]. It's usually tacit [src p. 354].
- It is not cohesiveness, permissiveness, or constant positivity; it's confidence that the team won't embarrass, reject, or punish someone for speaking up, rooted in mutual respect and trust [src p. 354]. It goes beyond trust [src pp. 354, 375].
- It enables **learning behaviors**: seeking feedback, sharing information, asking for help, talking about errors, experimenting [src pp. 351, 353].
- Leaders influence it through coaching, non-defensive responses to questions and challenges, and modeling learning behaviors themselves [src p. 356].
- It develops through a team's history of how risks were received [src p. 379].
- Limits: one company, 51 teams, cross-sectional, so causality isn't shown [src p. 379]; construct validity needs further work [src p. 378].

**What fairly counts as alignment in one workshop** [guidance]
- Facilitator choices meant to lower interpersonal risk: modeling not-knowing or admitting a mistake, a topic with no single right answer, responding to questions non-defensively, normalizing AI errors.
- Observed learning behaviors: participants asked questions, admitted confusion, or shared what didn't work.

**Avoid**
- Claiming a session "created psychological safety." It's a shared team belief built over time [src pp. 354, 379]. Say the design *supported* it.
- Equating it with comfort or niceness [src p. 354].
- Treating it as an individual trait or a one-to-one relationship. It's a climate the group shares [src pp. 354–355].
- Forgetting the original setting: work teams, not one-off workshop groups. Adapt carefully [guidance].
