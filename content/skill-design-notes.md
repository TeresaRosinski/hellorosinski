# Workshop write-up skill: design notes (from building the gold example)

## Fixed structure
1. Section intro (once, for the program): program name · years · one or two sentences
2. Per workshop:
   - Title + one-line promise
   - Metadata line: format (live/self-paced, virtual/in person) · Month Year · duration · participants · audience · tool
   - The situation
   - Who was in the room
   - The challenge (optional: only if it adds something the title and metadata don't)
   - Design choices (bold label + what I did + why)
   - Results
   - Learning foundations (3–4 theories max, each tied to a specific design choice)
   - See it (artifacts / links)
   - What I'd do next
3. If a case study can be opened as its own page, add a pointer back to the program section.

## Interviewer: questions that unlocked real content
- What was the situation before? Whose idea was it?
- Who was in the room (roles, levels, number, format)?
- How did you know that? Observed, heard, or assumed?
- What's your usual practice, and what did you change this time?
- How many finished? What did you actually observe?
- What happened afterwards (quotes, requests, later evidence)?
- What would you change next time?
- What artifacts exist that can be shared publicly?

## Voice guide
- Warm, confident, direct, clear, kind, capable, empowering
- Lead with the point; no throat-clearing openers
- No hedges or intensifiers ("I believe", "really", "greatly")
- Don't say things twice
- One vivid image at most
- Paraphrase internal leadership quotes; keep the company unnamed
- Intros answer "what is this, and who did it?" in the first sentence; one idea per sentence
- Session-specific details belong in that session's case study, not the program intro

## Reviewer rubrics: lessons
- L&D practitioner (most valuable): check theories are applied correctly (e.g. Prosci: "what's in it for me" = Desire, not Awareness; resources = support, not Reinforcement). Flag sentences that could describe any workshop. Flag claimed skills the design didn't actually teach.
- Hiring manager: 10-second skim first; plain-language test (could a reader with no AI/L&D background follow every sentence?); flag filler bullets; ask for one artifact.
- Skeptic: the author's first-person account counts as evidence; flag only claims a reader would actually doubt. Flag universal claims and narrow their scope. If a result rests on one story, tell the story; don't generalize. Flag intent stated as fact ("designed around X") and feelings stated as fact.
- Everyone: when data doesn't exist, don't invent it. Use a concrete detail or an artifact instead.
- Check public artifacts for participant names, internal ideas, or company details.
- Synthesizer keeps a flag checklist: every reviewer flag is marked fixed, kept on purpose (with the reason), or rejected (with the reason). Before calling a draft final, re-check the text against the list; nothing is silently dropped.

## Learning-theory alignment skill (planned, standalone)
- Curated list: Knowles adult learning, experiential learning (Kolb), self-determination theory, cognitive load / worked examples, scaffolding, social learning, ADKAR, Bloom's.
- Claim alignment ("aligned with", never "designed around" unless intentional) only with a specific matching design choice; rate strong / partial; say which don't apply.
- Use both ways: analyze finished workshops, and check new designs for gaps.
